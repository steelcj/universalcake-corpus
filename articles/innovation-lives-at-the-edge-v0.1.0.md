# Innovation Lives at the Edge

Version: 0.1.0
Status: Draft
License: CC BY-SA 4.0

## Abstract

The conventional wisdom in technology is to design for the centre and call it efficiency. This article argues, drawing on ecology, inclusive design research, and twenty-five years of evidence, that the strongest systems come from the opposite direction — from designing for the people the industry has learned to ignore.

---

## The comfortable centre

Most technology evaluation optimises for performance measured against the immediate needs of whoever is already being served. The target market is defined. The features are prioritised. The 80/20 rule is invoked. The edges are cut.

This produces systems that work well for a narrow group and fail in ways their builders never anticipated — because their builders never had access to the perspectives that would have revealed the failure modes.

The comfortable centre of any system is well-optimised. It is also the most fragile. It has never been asked to cope with conditions outside its design parameters. When those conditions arrive — and they always do — the centre fails first.

## The ecotone effect

An ecotone is the transitional zone between two ecosystems — the place where forest meets meadow, where river meets land. Odum (1953) first described what he called the edge effect: increased species richness and abundance in boundary regions, along with the presence of species found nowhere else.

Species living in ecotones are constantly adapting to the dynamic conditions of life in the in-between, which makes them especially resilient to change. They have developed capabilities the centre never needed because the centre was never asked to develop them.

The same pattern holds in human communities. People who have had to navigate between worlds develop greater idea receptiveness and creative potential than those who have inhabited only one (Leung & Chiu, 2010). Veblen (1919) observed the same dynamic in what he called hyphenate status — living simultaneously between two communities, belonging fully to neither. That position produces a quality of critical and creative thought that insiders cannot access because they cannot see what they are standing inside.

## The Virtuous Tornado

Jutta Treviranus (2019) at OCAD University put a name on the dynamic that emerges when design takes the edges seriously.

The conventional reading of Pareto tells developers to serve the easy 80% and ignore the difficult 20% at the margins. Treviranus points out that this is both a misreading of Pareto and a wrong turn. The people at the margins — the vital few — actually cover 80% of the knowledge terrain. Designing for them does not cost you. It repays you in knowledge, understanding, and systems that reach the edges of human diversity.

The Virtuous Tornado is the continuous, iterative process of asking "who are we missing?" and expanding inclusive design practices to reach them. Each cycle brings in perspectives the system did not previously have. Each new perspective reveals failure modes the system did not know about. Each fix strengthens the system for everyone, not just the people at the edges. The system grows stronger with each cycle.

This is not charity. It is where the strongest systems come from.

## The evidence from 1999

In 1999, Christopher Steel needed website software for a community that included people with disabilities, people who did not read or write English, and people using older hardware and operating systems. He wrote a checklist of qualities that mattered most. Accessibility moved to the top — not as a political statement but as a logical one. Without the door, the rest of the house is irrelevant.

Evaluating the options against that list, he found Plone. It was not finished. But its commitments were clear: multilingual, open source, web-editable, with accessibility treated as a first-class concern. Out-of-the-box Plone installations in the early 2000s met WCAG 1.0 AAA — the highest level under the standard in force at the time — making it one of the very few mainstream content management systems to achieve that distinction.

Plone's design went on to influence Wikipedia's MonoBook skin, which was the default face of the world's largest encyclopedia for six years. The organisations that adopted Plone — the United Nations, the FBI, governments on multiple continents — had exactly the same requirements as that original checklist. They needed systems that worked across languages, across abilities, and across decades.

Most of the software that competed with Plone in 1999 and 2000 is gone. Plone is still here. The list worked. And it worked because it started at the edge.

## The two directions

The research on edge communities runs in two directions simultaneously, and the honest version of this argument must hold both.

**The harm is real.** Minority stress — the chronic, socially-based stress experienced by marginalised groups through stigma, discrimination, and exclusion — produces measurable, serious health disparities (Meyer, 2003). The biopsychosocial effects of social stress shape rates of biological aging through inflammatory processes (Brown et al., 2020). Economic losses from excluding persons with disabilities from the labour force range from 3 to 7 per cent of GDP (Buckup, 2009). People are less productive not because they are "disabled" but because they live in environments that are disabling.

**The resilience is also real.** Post-traumatic growth — positive psychological changes that emerge from the struggle with significant adversity — produces new understandings, stronger relationships, and capabilities that would never have developed without the challenge (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996). Edge communities develop these capacities out of necessity. They learn to adapt, to read systems from the outside, to find solutions in the gaps between what was designed for them and what they actually need.

This is not a reason to celebrate exclusion or to treat suffering as a productive condition. The growth is real. The harm is real. And the harm that preceded the growth was neither inevitable nor acceptable.

The argument for designing from the edge is not that adversity is good. It is that the knowledge, resilience, and capability that edge communities have developed — at enormous personal cost — is exactly what the centre needs and does not have. Designing with those communities, not for them, produces systems that are stronger, more resilient, and more innovative than anything the centre produces alone.

## The reinforcing loops

Systems thinking reveals why edge-first design compounds rather than diminishes.

**The accessibility reinforcing loop.** Better accessibility expands participation. Wider participation attracts more contributors. More contributors improve documentation. Better documentation strengthens accessibility. The loop runs in both directions — exclude people and it degrades with equal force.

**The language infrastructure loop.** Better language infrastructure enables more communities to engage. More communities generate more diverse knowledge. Diverse knowledge strengthens the system. A stronger system can invest in better language infrastructure. A system launched in one language, optimised for one community, is running this loop in reverse.

**The bridging capital loop.** Organisations that reach beyond their known supporters build bridging capital (Putnam, 2000), which expands the pool of participants, increases diversity of perspectives, enables better exploration (March, 1991), and builds more bridging capital. The organisations that only work the known turf eventually run out of it.

All three loops produce the same structural finding: designing for the edges does not subtract from the centre. It strengthens the entire system.

---

## The shift

The conventional approach builds for the centre and hopes the edges will adapt. Universal Cake builds for the edges and watches the centre benefit from what is learned there.

This is not idealism. It is the design logic that produced the systems still standing after twenty-five years, while the ones that optimised for the comfortable majority have disappeared.

From capability to consequence. From the centre of the bell curve to its edges. From the short list to the full picture.

> The list Christopher made in 1999 was not unusual. It was just an honest assessment of what humans actually need from technology.
