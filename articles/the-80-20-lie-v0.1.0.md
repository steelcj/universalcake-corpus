# The 80/20 Lie: How a Rule About Italian Land Ownership Became a Licence to Ignore Most of Humanity

Version: 0.1.0
Status: Draft
License: CC BY-SA 4.0

## Abstract

The technology industry's most pervasive design principle is based on a misreading of a nineteenth-century observation about wealth concentration in Italy. Applied to software, the 80/20 rule became a licence to ignore anyone not already well-served. This article examines how that misapplication produces compounded digital exclusion — and why designing for the edges is not charity but the source of the strongest systems we have.

---

## The rule that was never a rule

Vilfredo Pareto was an Italian economist working in the 1890s. His original observation was very specific: approximately 80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of its population. It was a descriptive statistic about wealth concentration in one country at one moment in history.

It was not a magic formula. It was not a universal law of nature. It was not a design principle, not an instruction, not a rule. Even that basic statistic does not hold universally today.

Yet it became all three to many of the people responsible for creating the software we rely on. Ironically, as Treviranus (2019) notes, Pareto had nothing to do with the development of the term that would come to bear his name. Literally nothing at all other than the percentages 80% and 20%.

Applied to technological design, the 80/20 rule became a licence to ignore anyone who was not already well-served — to treat a comfortable majority as the only audience worth building for, and to call that efficiency.

---

## The 80% was never the world's 80%

The problem is that the "80%" the principle describes was never the world's 80%. It was the 80% of a very specific and narrow market — usually English-speaking consumers with high bandwidth and recent hardware — that was considered included before a technological project even began.

The actual numbers tell a different story:

- Around **380 million** people speak English as their native language — roughly **5%** of the world's population (Eberhard et al., 2024)
- Even including everyone who speaks English as a second language, **more than 4 in 5 people** in the world do not understand English
- Yet **54%** of websites worldwide are in English (Statista, 2024)
- There are some **7,170 living languages** in the world; roughly **44%** are now endangered, often with fewer than 1,000 users remaining

The technology industry did not design for 80% of the world. It designed for a small minority of it, gave that minority a name — "the majority" — and then applied a rule about Italian land ownership from the 1890s to justify ignoring everyone else.

Every one of those 7,170 languages represents a community of people, a way of understanding the world, a body of knowledge that exists nowhere else. The 80/20 rule, as applied to technology, has nothing to say about any of them. It cannot even see them.

## When every product ignores the same 20%

There is a second major blind spot that is harder to see but significantly more damaging in practice.

It is not just that any one product ignores 20% of its potential users. It is what happens when every product does this independently, each to its own version of the "majority."

Consider a person who speaks a minority language, lives in a low-income household, uses older hardware, has low or intermittent bandwidth, and has a visual impairment. Each of these characteristics places them outside the target market of a different set of products and services:

- The **banking app** is designed for smartphone users on current hardware
- The **government portal** is available only in the dominant language
- The **healthcare platform** requires a screen resolution the older device cannot produce
- The **job search tool** assumes broadband speeds that are not available in the neighbourhood

Each product, in isolation, made a reasonable-sounding decision to serve its majority. But the cumulative effect on this one person is not a 20% reduction in access. It is near-total exclusion from the digital infrastructure on which modern life increasingly depends.

Researchers call this **compounded digital exclusion** — the phenomenon in which digital inequalities do not operate along independent axes of division, but overlap, interlink and interact, producing outcomes far more severe than any single exclusion would suggest (Zheng & Walsham, 2021).

When the 80/20 rule is applied independently across thousands of products and services, each drawing its target market from roughly the same centre of the bell curve, the people at the edges are not excluded from one thing. They are excluded from most things. The exclusions stack.

This is not a marginal problem. The people most likely to be at the edges of multiple target markets simultaneously are also the people who most depend on technology for access to vital services — healthcare, education, employment, government, banking, communication. The 80/20 rule, applied at scale, does not produce a world where 80% of people are well-served and 20% are somewhat underserved. It produces a world where the same people are comprehensively excluded from nearly everything, while the comfortable centre is served by an abundance of competing options.

## The measurable cost

Exclusion is not abstract. It is measurable.

Economic losses from excluding persons with disabilities from the labour force range from **3 to 7 per cent of GDP** (Buckup, 2009). Critically, people with disabilities are less productive not because they are "disabled" but because they live and work in environments that are **disabling**.

The cost is not in the people. It is in the systems that exclude them.

Chronic social stress, the kind produced by sustained exclusion, shapes rates of biological aging through inflammatory processes (Brown et al., 2020). This is not metaphor. It is measurable physiology. Living at the edge, under conditions of exclusion, makes people sick.

Beyond the direct economic losses, the social and political costs compound: exclusion narrows the range of voices shaping decisions, concentrates power in the centre, and produces systems that serve an ever-smaller slice of the population while accumulating the costs of everyone left out.

## Innovation lives at the edge

There is a counterargument to the 80/20 rule that goes beyond ethics. It is about where the best systems actually come from.

**The ecotone effect.** An ecotone is the transitional zone between two ecosystems — where forest meets meadow, where river meets land. Odum (1953) described the edge effect: increased species richness and abundance in boundary regions, along with the presence of species found nowhere else. The comfortable centre of any ecosystem is well-optimised. It is also the most fragile. The edges have been asked to cope. They have developed capabilities the centre never needed.

**The Virtuous Tornado.** Treviranus (2019) argues that the people at the margins cover 80% of the knowledge terrain. Designing for them repays you in knowledge, understanding, and systems that reach the edges of human diversity. The continuous, iterative process of asking *who are we missing* and expanding inclusive design to reach them creates systems that grow stronger with each cycle.

**The bridging capital effect.** March (1991) describes the difference between exploitation — strengthening what you already have — and exploration — building across new boundaries. Organisations that only work the known turf eventually run out of it. The ones that invest in the edges are the ones that grow.

**Plone validated this in 1999.** Built for accessibility, multilingual, open source. Adopted by the United Nations, the FBI, and governments on multiple continents. Most of its competitors are gone. Plone is still here. The organisations that chose it had the same requirements as the original checklist: systems that worked across languages, across abilities, and across decades. The list was not unusual. It was just honest.

## 80% of whom, exactly?

The idea of having a target market is not wrong in every context. Applied carefully, within a clearly defined and honestly described market, it can be a tool for prioritisation. But it requires honesty about who that market actually is, and who it is not.

When the target market is "English speakers on modern hardware with fast internet connections," the rule describes that market. It says nothing about the majority of humanity other than: we do not acknowledge or see you.

Universal Cake does not pretend the 80/20 rule does not exist. It insists on asking: **80% of whom, exactly?** And what happens if we leave out everyone else? Is this efficiency? Is this helpful? Is this sustainable? Is this a good investment?

The answers, increasingly, are no.

---

## The full picture

Universal Cake asks designers, developers, and decision-makers to hold the full picture in mind — not just the target market for a particular product, but the cumulative effect that target markets have on the people who fall outside most of them.

The word *universal* describes the goal: technology that works for the widest possible range of people, languages, abilities, devices, and circumstances. Not technology designed for the comfortable centre, but technology that keeps asking — in the words of Treviranus (2019) — "who are we missing?" and works hard to find real answers.

That shift — from capability to consequence, from the centre of the bell curve to its edges, from the short list to the full picture — is the heart of the Universal Cake framework.
