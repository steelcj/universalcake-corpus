# Universal Cake -- An Introduction

Version: 0.1.1
Status: Draft

## Abstract

This document introduces Universal Cake, a framework for thinking about technology in human terms. It explains where the idea came from, what the name means, and how it can help anyone — technical or not — ask better questions about the tools they use every day.

## Inspirations

Universal Cake began in 1999 as a personal checklist. Christopher Steel was looking for web software and wrote down the questions that mattered to him: Does it come in my language? Does it work on my system? Does it work in the browsers my users have? Is it accessible to people with disabilities? Is it supported? Is it maintained? Does it have the qualities needed for longevity? Is it worth investing in? That list was not borrowed from anywhere. It came from experience — from working with real people who were being left out by technology that was not designed with them in mind.

Over time, that checklist grew into a framework. And over time, Christopher encountered others who had been asking similar questions from their own directions. Some of this is prior art — thinking that existed before that checklist was written, though he was not aware of it at the time. Some of it is new art — parallel thinking that has emerged since.

<a name="apa-meadows-citation"></a>[Meadows (2008)](#apa-meadows-reference) and her work on systems thinking is a recent and satisfying discovery: the idea that healthy systems are made of interdependent parts that support one another maps directly onto the layers of the cake.

The human inspiration behind Universal Cake is wide and does not fit neatly into a reference list. It spans disability rights — including the work of <a name="apa-treviranus-citation"></a>[Jutta Treviranus at OCAD University](#apa-treviranus-reference), Catherine Roy at McGill University, and the advocacy of <a name="apa-rapliq-citation"></a>[RAPLIQ](#apa-rapliq-reference) — alongside philosophy, systems thinking, trauma-informed practice, and personal resilience. People like bell hooks, Donella Meadows, Pema Chödrön, Ed Roberts, Ted Nelson, and Simone de Beauvoir have all shaped how Christopher thinks about people, systems, and what it means to build something that serves rather than excludes. Technology does not exist apart from these questions. It is shaped by them, and shapes them in return.

The plain language principles used in this document follow the <a name="apa-plain-language-citation"></a>[Plain Language Action and Information Network (2011)](#apa-plain-language-reference).

## 1. Where it started

In 1999, Christopher Steel set out to find web software that could do something deceptively simple: work for the people in his community. Not just for English speakers. Not just for people with fast computers and perfect eyesight. Not just for the organisations that could afford the most expensive tools. For as wide an audience as possible.

That search turned out to be harder than expected. Most software was built to be fast, profitable, or popular. Very little of it was built to be fair, durable, or kind. The question of who gets left out rarely was rarely even discussed and almost never manifested on feature lists.

As and individual living with disability, Chris realized that unlike many geographical barriers, technology, like choosing to build stairs rather than ramps was purely in the hands of humans, not only that, but it was actually significantly easier to create and maintain accessible technological infrastructure then physical objects like homes and offices that where accessible from the gorund up.

If humans created it, then humans had agency and the ability to make technology work better for all humans. I fact , imagining the systems in place today at the time being accessibile vs. inaccessible avoided a huge number of social and political disasters not to mention suffering. Yet here we are building exclusive technology that leaves out a huge percentage of the population as well has restricting the human sphere of potential knowledge (Jutta)

That rapidly expanding gap between what technology and human ingenuity were capable of, the worlds actual needs and the short sighted misinterpretation of 80/20 rules approach to producing technology became the starting point for the ideas behind creating Universal Cake.

## 2. What the name means

The word *universal* describes the goal: technology that works for the widest possible range of people, languages, abilities, devices, and circumstances. Not technology for the average user. Technology that tries to leave no one behind. (Jutta, virtuous tornado)

McLuhen - extended human nervous system

The word *cake* describes the dessert that everyone loves to eat. The ingredients for a delicious cake for everyone include things like accessibility, sustainability, transparency, resilience, and human well-being.

No single ingredient is enough on its own, it takes all of them to create something truly marvellous.

## 3. The core idea

Technology is created by humans directly and indirectly. When we have agency we can create it in ways that allow it to improve accessibility and usability.

Technology is not neutral. Every tool makes choices. Those choices affect who can use it, how long it lasts, who controls it, and what it does to the people around it.

Universal Cake proposes a simple shift: stop asking only "does it work?" and start asking are we building this in a way that allows us to increase inclusions by supporting human needs out of the box. This is the only way that our systems and services will work for people — as many of them as possible, over longer periods of time.

That shift changes everything. A note-taking app is no longer just fast or slow. It is also asking: can I get my notes out if the app creator company shuts down? Can someone using a screen reader actually use my website? Will my notes be readable in ten years? These are not technical questions. They are human ones.

## 4. What Universal Cake evaluates

Universal Cake can be applied to almost any technology — software, hardware, infrastructure, documentation systems, or communication platforms. It evaluates them across a set of human-centered dimensions rather than purely technical ones.

**Accessibility** asks whether diverse people can actually use the system — including people living with disabilities, people using older hardware, people with slow internet connections, and people who speak languages other than English.

**Sustainability** asks whether the system can last. This includes environmental costs like energy use, but also human costs: does it burn out the people who maintain it? Does it require constant expensive upgrades?

**Interoperability** asks whether your data can move freely. Can you take your work elsewhere if you need to? Does the system use open standards that other tools can read, or does it lock you in?

**Transparency** asks whether you can understand what the system is doing. Can you inspect it? Can you audit it? Or is it a black box that you simply have to trust?

**Resilience** asks whether the system can survive disruption — a company closing, an internet outage, a change in political environment. Can it keep working when things go wrong?

**Human well-being** asks what the system does to the people who use it. Does it reduce stress or increase it? Does it support healthy habits or undermine them? Does it respect human attention and limitation?

**Knowledge preservation** asks whether the information stored in the system will remain readable in the future. Will your files open in twenty years? Does the system depend on a proprietary format that could disappear?

No technology scores perfectly across all of these. The goal is not perfection. The goal is honest, balanced evaluation — and a commitment to doing better.

## 5. Why this matters now

Most technology today is evaluated on a short list of criteria: speed, features, price, and popularity. These are not bad criteria. But they are incomplete.

They do not ask who gets excluded. They do not ask what happens when the company behind the tool changes direction or closes. They do not ask what the tool does to the mental health of the people who use it every day. They do not ask whether the knowledge stored in the tool will survive the next decade.

Universal Cake does not reject the short list. It extends it. It argues that technology which ignores human sustainability is not truly advanced — it is just fast in the short term.

## 6. Who Universal Cake is for

Universal Cake is for anyone who uses technology and wants to make better choices about it. That includes individuals choosing a note-taking app, organisations selecting infrastructure, educators building learning environments, and governments procuring public systems.

It is also for builders. Developers, designers, and architects who want to think more carefully about what they are creating and who it serves — and who it leaves behind.

The framework does not require technical expertise to use. Its most important questions are human ones.

## 7. How to use Universal Cake

The simplest way to use Universal Cake is to ask the guiding questions whenever you are evaluating a tool or building a system.

- Who can use this? Who cannot?
- Will this still work in ten years?
- Can I take my data with me if I leave?
- Can I understand what this system is doing?
- Does this system support or undermine the well-being of the people who use it?
- What happens if the organisation behind it disappears?
- Does it work for people with different abilities, languages, and devices?

These questions do not always have easy answers. But asking them changes the conversation. They shift the focus from "what can this do?" to "what does this do to people?" — and that shift is the heart of Universal Cake.
