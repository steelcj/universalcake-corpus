# Universal Cake -- An Introduction

## Abstract

This document introduces Universal Cake, a framework for thinking about technology in human terms. It explains where the idea came from, what the name means, and how it can help anyone — technical or not, ask better questions about the tools they use every day.

## Sources and acknowledgements

The Universal Cake framework was developed by <a name="apa-steel-mission-citation"></a>[Christopher Steel](#apa-steel-mission-reference) over a period of more than twenty-five years, beginning with a software evaluation practice established in 1999. Today, the framework draws on multiple fields.  <a name="apa-norman-citation"></a>[Norman (2013)](#apa-norman-reference) and on accessibility standards defined by the <a name="apa-wcag-citation"></a>[W3C Web Accessibility Initiative (2023)](#apa-wcag-reference). The plain language principles used in this document follow the <a name="apa-plain-language-citation"></a>[Plain Language Action and Information Network (2011)](#apa-plain-language-reference).

## 1. Where it started

In 1999, Christopher Steel set out to find web software that could do something deceptively simple: work for everyone. Not just for English speakers. Not just for people with fast computers and perfect eyesight. Not just for the organisation that could afford the most expensive tools. For everyone.

That search turned out to be harder than expected. Most software was built to be fast, profitable, or popular. Very little of it was built to be fair, durable, or kind. The question of who gets left out rarely appeared in the feature list.

That gap — between what technology could do and what it actually did for most people — became the starting point for Universal Cake.

## 2. What the name means

The word *universal* describes the goal: technology that works for the widest possible range of people, languages, abilities, devices, and circumstances. Not technology for the average user. Technology that tries to leave no one behind.

The word *cake* describes the structure. A cake is made of layers. Each layer depends on the others. If one layer fails completely, the whole thing suffers. Universal Cake uses this image to describe how healthy technology systems are built — not as single features, but as many interconnected qualities working together.

Those layers include things like accessibility, sustainability, transparency, resilience, and human well-being. No single layer is enough on its own. A system can be fast and still be fragile. It can be popular and still be harmful. Universal Cake asks about all the layers at once.

## 3. The core idea

Technology is not neutral. Every tool makes choices. Those choices affect who can use it, how long it lasts, who controls it, and what it does to the people around it.

Universal Cake proposes a simple shift: stop asking only "does it work?" and start asking "does it work for people — all people, over a long period of time?"

That shift changes everything. A note-taking app is no longer just fast or slow. It is also asking: can I get my notes out if the company shuts down? Can someone using a screen reader use it? Will my notes be readable in ten years? These are not technical questions. They are human ones.

## 4. What Universal Cake evaluates

Universal Cake can be applied to almost any technology — software, hardware, infrastructure, documentation systems, or communication platforms. It evaluates them across a set of human-centered dimensions rather than purely technical ones.

**Accessibility** asks whether diverse people can actually use the system — including people living with disabilities, people using older hardware, people with slow internet connections, and people who speak languages other than English.

**Sustainability** asks whether the system can last. This includes environmental costs like energy use, but also human costs: does it burn out the people who maintain it? Does it require constant expensive upgrades?

**Interoperability** asks whether your data can move freely. Can you take your work elsewhere if you need to? Does the system use open standards that other tools can read, or does it lock you in?

**Transparency** asks whether you can understand what the system is doing. Can you inspect it? Can you audit it? Or is it a black box that you simply have to trust?

**Resilience** asks whether the system can survive disruption — a company closing, an internet outage, a change in political environment. Can it keep working when things go wrong?

**Human well-being** asks what the system does to the people who use it. Does it reduce stress or increase it? Does it support healthy habits or undermine them? Does it respect human attention and limitation?

**Knowledge preservation** asks whether the information stored in the system will remain readable in the future. Will your files open in twenty years? Does the system depend on a proprietary format that could disappear?

No technology scores perfectly across all of these. The goal is not perfection. The goal is honest, balanced evaluation — and a commitment to doing better.

## 5. Why this matters now

Most technology today is evaluated on a short list of criteria: speed, features, price, and popularity. These are not bad criteria. But they are incomplete.

They do not ask who gets excluded. They do not ask what happens when the company behind the tool changes direction or closes. They do not ask what the tool does to the mental health of the people who use it every day. They do not ask whether the knowledge stored in the tool will survive the next decade.

Universal Cake does not reject the short list. It extends it. It argues that technology which ignores human sustainability is not truly advanced — it is just fast in the short term.

## 6. Who Universal Cake is for

Universal Cake is for anyone who uses technology and wants to make better choices about it. That includes individuals choosing a note-taking app, organisations selecting infrastructure, educators building learning environments, and governments procuring public systems.

It is also for builders. Developers, designers, and architects who want to think more carefully about what they are creating and who it serves — and who it leaves behind.

The framework does not require technical expertise to use. Its most important questions are human ones.

## 7. How to use Universal Cake

The simplest way to use Universal Cake is to ask the guiding questions whenever you are evaluating a tool or building a system.

- Who can use this? Who cannot?
- Will this still work in ten years?
- Can I take my data with me if I leave?
- Can I understand what this system is doing?
- Does this system support or undermine the well-being of the people who use it?
- What happens if the organisation behind it disappears?
- Does it work for people with different abilities, languages, and devices?

These questions do not always have easy answers. But asking them changes the conversation. They shift the focus from "what can this do?" to "what does this do to people?" — and that shift is the heart of Universal Cake.
