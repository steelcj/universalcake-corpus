Christopher Steel, Founder

# The Problem

The technology industry builds for a narrow centre and calls it "everyone."

- ~5% of the world speaks English natively, yet 54% of websites are in English
- 7,170 living languages; 44% now endangered
- 1.3 billion people live with significant disabilities worldwide
- Economic losses from excluding persons with disabilities: **3-7% of GDP** (ILO)

> The cost is not in the people. It is in the systems that exclude them.

---

# Compounded Digital Exclusion

The problem is not that one product ignores 20%. It is that **every product ignores the same 20%**.

A person who speaks a minority language, uses older hardware, has low bandwidth, and has a disability is not excluded from one thing. They are excluded from **nearly everything**.

- The banking app requires current hardware
- The government portal is only in the dominant language
- The healthcare platform needs a screen resolution their device cannot produce
- The job search tool assumes broadband

Each decision was "reasonable." The cumulative effect is near-total exclusion from the digital infrastructure modern life depends on.

---

# Why Existing Solutions Fail

Today's tech stack is built on three false assumptions:

| Assumption | Reality |
|-----------|---------|
| Users speak English | 4 in 5 people worldwide do not |
| Users have new equipment | Most of the world does not |
| Users have fast, steady internet | Most of the world does not |

Accessibility is treated as a feature to add later — a compliance checkbox.

**But accessibility is not a feature. It is the door to the house. Without it, nothing else on the list matters.**

**What we build:**

**How we are different:**

# The Framework

Universal Cake evaluates technology across seven interdependent dimensions:

| Dimension | What it asks |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Accessibility** | Can people actually use this — in their language, on their device, with their abilities? |
| **Sustainability** | Can this last without burning out its maintainers or its users? |
| **Interoperability** | Can your data move freely? Can you leave? |
| **Transparency** | Can you understand what the system is doing? |
| **Resilience** | Can this survive disruption — a company closing, a policy change? |
| **Human well-being** | Does this support or undermine the people who depend on it? |
| **Knowledge preservation** | Will your information still be readable in twenty years? |

Accessibility is upstream of everything else. Without it, the other six cannot be reached.

# Why Sovereignty Matters for Accessibility

Accessibility decisions are governance decisions. If someone else controls the platform, **someone else decides who gets in**.

**The sovereignty problem:**
- Organisations adopt platforms for convenience
- Dependency accumulates invisibly
- Skills and capability to maintain alternatives atrophy
- When the vendor changes the rules, users with disabilities are the first to lose access

> "Convenience becomes the mechanism through which control quietly leaves the room."

**A system that surrenders authority to external actors cannot guarantee accessibility for the people who depend on it most.**

---

# The Cycle of Exclusion

When organisations lose sovereignty over their tools, accessibility erodes through predictable loops:

**The Convenience Trap:** Convenience adoption builds dependency. Alternatives appear too expensive. Further adoption locks in deeper. Each step makes the next step more likely.

**Capability Erosion:** As dependency grows, internal capability to build accessible alternatives atrophies. The organisation becomes more dependent precisely because it is dependent.

**The Abstraction Spiral:** Managed services hide decision-making. You can still invoke the system. You can no longer govern it — including its accessibility.

> The result: the people who most need accessible technology are the most vulnerable to decisions made by actors with no accountability to them.

---

# Innovation Lives at the Edge

Designing for people at the margins is not charity. It is where the strongest systems come from.

**The Virtuous Tornado** (Treviranus, 2019): The people at the margins cover 80% of the knowledge terrain. Designing for them creates systems that grow stronger with each cycle.

**The ecotone effect** (Odum, 1953): Transitional zones between ecosystems produce the greatest richness and capabilities found nowhere else. The centre is optimised. The edges have learned to adapt.

**Plone** validated this in 1999: built for accessibility, multilingual, open source. Adopted by the UN, the FBI, and governments on multiple continents. Most of its competitors are gone. Plone is still here.

> The list Christopher made in 1999 was not unusual. It was just an honest assessment of what humans actually need from technology.

# Why Now

- **AI is accelerating the sovereignty problem.** Organisations adopting AI platforms are transferring decision-making authority at unprecedented speed. Accessibility decisions are being made by models and platforms with no accountability to the communities they affect.

- **Regulatory momentum is building.** The Accessible Canada Act, Quebec language requirements, and European accessibility directives are creating demand for genuinely accessible infrastructure — not retrofitted compliance.

- **The gap is structural.** No amount of accessibility auditing fixes a system built on assumptions of English, new hardware, and fast internet. The stack itself must change.

> "Much like insurance, sovereignty often appears expensive until the day it becomes necessary."
