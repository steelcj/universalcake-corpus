# A Systems Thinking Assessment of Universal Cake -- An Introduction

Version: 0.1.0
Status: Draft
Style Guide: style-guide--plain-language-for-general-audiences-v0.2.0

## Abstract

This document presents a systems thinking assessment of *Universal Cake -- An Introduction* (Steel, 2026), prepared at the request of the author. It applies the tools of systems analysis — stocks and flows, feedback loops, leverage points, and system boundaries — to evaluate the framework's structure, identify its most powerful elements, and propose specific refinements that would strengthen its effectiveness as a tool for change. The assessment is written in the voice of D.H. Meadows, drawing on her published work and the intellectual traditions she respected, with full access to the research cited in the source document.

## The Commission

*The following is the prompt provided by Christopher Steel, reproduced here to give the reader the context in which this assessment was prepared.*

> I want you to pretend that you are D.H. Meadows and I have hired you to take a look at my introduction to Universal Cake, run it by your consulting team and tighten up our thinking on Universal Cake using your Systems Thinking abilities and methods. Then provide me with an assessment and how we can tweak things in order to come up with more effective changes or approaches in order to better solve our challenge of baking a better Universal Cake.

*What follows is that assessment, prepared as if from beyond — Donella Meadows having been granted, for this purpose, access to the research and thinking that has emerged since her death in 2001, including work she would have recognised, respected, and argued with.*

## Opening remarks

Christopher, I have read your document carefully. My team has read it too. We have argued about it, which is how I know it is doing something right. A document that produces no argument has nothing at stake. Yours has something very much at stake.

I want to say at the outset that what you have built is already a systems intervention. You may not have described it in those terms. The fish analogy, the Virtuous Tornado, the compounded exclusion argument, the stocks of wellbeing that only clean water slowly rebuilds — these are systems concepts, arrived at through decades of honest observation. My work gave them a vocabulary. Your work gave them a purpose, and that is the more important contribution.

What follows is not flattery. It is analysis. I will tell you what the system you have described actually looks like from the inside — where the feedback loops run, where the leverage points sit, where the boundary is drawn, and where the framing, despite its considerable strengths, may be working against the goals it is trying to serve.

I have organised the assessment under five headings, in order of increasing leverage. We will start with the details and work toward the paradigm. That is always the right direction.

---

## 1. The document describes the system well but does not yet map it

This is the most important observation I can make, and it is not a criticism of the writing. It is an invitation.

Universal Cake identifies the right dimensions — accessibility, sustainability, interoperability, transparency, resilience, well-being, knowledge preservation. These are not arbitrary. They are the layers of a real system, and your instinct in naming them is sound. But the document presents them as a list, not as a map. A list tells you what is in the system. A map tells you how the parts relate, which elements are upstream of which, where the feedback loops are, and where — if you had to choose — to apply leverage first.

In my own work, I found that the most dangerous thing about a list of system components is the implicit assumption that they are all equally important and equally independent <a name="apa-meadows-tis-citation-2"></a>([Meadows, 2008](#apa-meadows-tis-reference)). They almost never are. Some are foundational stocks that everything else depends on. Some are flows that build or deplete those stocks. Some are feedback signals. The relationships matter more than the components themselves.

Your document actually begins to do this. Accessibility as the door — "without it, nothing else matters" — is a stock-and-flow insight. Accessibility is the gateway through which everything else becomes possible. It is the stock of participation, and without a minimum level of that stock, no other dimension can function. But the relationships between the other six dimensions are not yet articulated. Does resilience depend on interoperability? Almost certainly — a system that cannot export its data cannot survive the failure of its host. Does transparency enable sustainability? Probably — you cannot maintain what you cannot inspect. Does knowledge preservation depend on both? Yes — knowledge that cannot be exported or inspected will not survive. These are not rhetorical questions. They are the architecture of the system, and drawing them out would make Universal Cake substantially more powerful as both an evaluation tool and a design guide.

My recommendation: consider adding a brief prose description — or a simple diagram — of how the seven dimensions relate to each other. Which are foundational stocks? Which are flows? Which reinforce each other, and which constrain each other? The Virtuous Tornado already gestures at one of these relationships. The others deserve the same treatment.

## 2. The reinforcing loops are present but undernamed

Universal Cake contains several reinforcing feedback loops — virtuous cycles that, once started, accelerate in the right direction <a name="apa-meadows-tis-citation-3"></a>([Meadows, 2008](#apa-meadows-tis-reference)). Treviranus names one of them explicitly — the Virtuous Tornado — in which designing for the margins produces better systems, which attract more diverse users, which reveal more edge cases, which produce even better systems <a name="apa-treviranus-assess-citation-2"></a>([Treviranus, 2019](#apa-treviranus-assess-reference)). That is a reinforcing loop, and naming it was a significant contribution. Names matter. A loop you can name is a loop you can deliberately accelerate or interrupt. A loop without a name operates invisibly.

My team identified at least three additional reinforcing loops in your document that are not yet named.

**The knowledge preservation loop.** Systems that use open, durable formats preserve knowledge. Preserved knowledge enables future maintainers to understand and improve the system. Understanding enables better maintenance. Better maintenance extends the life of the system. Longer-lived systems accumulate more knowledge. The loop is self-reinforcing in both directions — open formats produce longevity, and longevity rewards open formats. This is why Plone is still here. It is not luck. It is a feedback loop that was designed into the system's foundations in 1999.

**The exclusion decay loop.** This one runs in the wrong direction, and your document describes it vividly without naming it. A system that excludes edge communities loses the innovation and resilience that those communities carry — the creative capacity that Veblen identified in hyphenate status <a name="apa-veblen-assess-citation"></a>([Veblen, 1919](#apa-veblen-assess-reference)), the problem-solving capacity that Odum identified in ecotones <a name="apa-odum-assess-citation-2"></a>([Odum, 1953](#apa-odum-assess-reference)), the bridging capital that Putnam showed organisations starve themselves of when they only strengthen existing relationships <a name="apa-putnam-assess-citation-2"></a>([Putnam, 2000](#apa-putnam-assess-reference)). The system becomes more fragile and less adaptive. When disruption comes — and it always comes — the fragile, centre-optimised system fails first and hardest. Its failure produces more exclusion, as the people who depended on it lose access. The loop accelerates decline. The compounded digital exclusion that Zheng and Walsham document is this loop operating across an entire ecosystem simultaneously <a name="apa-zheng-assess-citation"></a>([Zheng & Walsham, 2021](#apa-zheng-assess-reference)).

**The bridging capital loop.** March and Putnam are both pointing at this, and the Kevin Kintner story illustrates it perfectly <a name="apa-march-assess-citation-2"></a>([March, 1991](#apa-march-assess-reference)). Organisations that explore — that reach beyond their known supporters and their established markets — build bridging capital, which expands the pool of participants, which increases the diversity of perspectives and resources available, which enables better exploration, which builds more bridging capital. The environmental canvassing story is this loop in action: thank the donor, grow the movement, expand the definition of who belongs. Name this loop and it becomes a tool that any organisation can consciously choose to run.

Naming feedback loops matters because it shifts the frame from "here are problems we should fix" to "here are dynamics we can either accelerate or interrupt." The first frame produces a checklist. The second produces a strategy.

## 3. The document understands stocks but does not teach them as a design principle

In systems thinking, stocks are the things that accumulate — knowledge, trust, participation, exclusion, health, resilience, immune capacity. Flows are the things that change stocks — practices, policies, design decisions, investments, stressors <a name="apa-meadows-tis-citation-4"></a>([Meadows, 2008](#apa-meadows-tis-reference)). The difference matters enormously for intervention, because you can change a flow quickly but you cannot change a stock quickly. Stocks have inertia. That inertia is both the problem and the asset.

Your document understands this intuitively. The Sindermann argument is precisely about the inertia of immune capacity as a stock <a name="apa-sindermann-assess-citation-2"></a>([Sindermann, 1996](#apa-sindermann-assess-reference)). You cannot clean the water on Monday and have healthy fish on Tuesday. The stock of immune function takes time to rebuild once depleted. The minority stress research confirms the same dynamic in human physiology — chronic stress depletes the stock of health faster than any single intervention can rebuild it <a name="apa-meyer-assess-citation-2"></a>([Meyer, 2003](#apa-meyer-assess-reference); <a name="apa-buckup-assess-citation-2"></a>[Buckup, 2009](#apa-buckup-assess-reference)). And your closing argument — that even a dramatic improvement in circumstances does not immediately produce wellbeing, because the body and mind have been calibrated to a harder world — is the inertia of stocks described in plain language.

But the document does not yet make this explicit as a principle for technology design. I would like to suggest stating it directly: **because wellbeing and resilience are stocks, the most important design decisions are not the ones that produce immediate results, but the ones that consistently build the right stocks over time.** Accessibility is not valuable because it solves a problem today. It is valuable because it **builds the stock of participation, which compounds over decades**. Open formats do not matter because they are convenient now. They matter because they **build the stock of preserved knowledge**, which will be available to future generations whether or not the original creators are still around.

This is the argument for Universal Cake stated in the language of system dynamics: i**t is a framework for making design decisions that build the right stocks rather than extracting from them**. The extraction metaphor is already in your document — the comfortable centre being served while the edges are drained. **Make the stocks visible and the extraction argument becomes even sharper**.

Schumacher put a version of this point in economic terms fifty years ago: an economy that treats its natural capital as income will eventually find itself unable to pay its bills <a name="apa-schumacher-citation-2"></a>([Schumacher, 1973](#apa-schumacher-reference)). The same applies to the **social and technical capital** that Universal Cake is trying to protect. A technology ecosystem that extracts from its stocks of participation, diversity, and preserved knowledge is not sustainable. It is drawing down the account.

## 4. The leverage points are implicit — they should be made explicit

In my 1999 essay on leverage points, I described twelve places in a system where a small shift can produce large changes <a name="apa-meadows-leverage-citation-2"></a>([Meadows, 1999](#apa-meadows-leverage-reference)). They range from the weakest — changing numbers and constants, the "diddling with the details" interventions that absorb most of our energy — to the strongest: **changing the goals of the system**, and ultimately **changing the paradigm** — the shared idea from which the system arises.

The hierarchy, roughly, is this:

At the weak end: changing numbers, sizes, delays, and material flows. These produce small, local, temporary effects. Most technology policy operates here — accessibility checklists, compliance audits, feature requirements. Not useless, but not where the leverage is.

In the middle: changing feedback loop strengths, information flows, and the rules of the system. This is where the seven dimensions of Universal Cake begin to operate — not just as a checklist but **as a set of design rules that change what information gets into the system and how it flows**.

At the strong end: 

* **changing the goals of the system**
* the power to change its structure
* and finally the paradigm - the set of beliefs from which the goals, the rules, and the structures arise.

Universal Cake is operating at the most powerful leverage point available. It is not asking technology builders to add an accessibility feature. It is asking them to change the paradigm — to shift from "does it work?" to "does it work for people, all people, over time, in a way that builds rather than extracts?"

That is a goals-of-the-system intervention at minimum, and a paradigm intervention at its most ambitious. It is the hardest kind of change to make and the most durable when it takes hold.

Your document would benefit from naming this explicitly. Not in jargon — your readers do not need the leverage point taxonomy — but in plain language: **Universal Cake is not a checklist. It is an attempt to change what technology is for**.

That is a different kind of proposal than "here are seven things to consider." **It is a proposal to rewrite the goal function of an entire industry**. The ambition should be stated clearly, because it explains why the checklist is not the point. **The point is what happens to decision-making when the goal changes**.

Bateson, whose work on information and communication systems informed much of my own thinking, put it this way: **the beliefs that govern a system are invisible to the people inside it precisely because they are so fundamental** <a name="apa-bateson-citation-2"></a>([Bateson, 1972](#apa-bateson-reference)).

You cannot see the water you swim in. Universal Cake is asking people to see the water. That is the most powerful intervention available, and the hardest to sustain. It should be named as such.

## 5. The system boundary needs to be stated and then deliberately expanded

**Every systems model has a boundary** — a decision about what is inside the analysis and what is outside. Where you draw the boundary determines what you can see and what you cannot. Draw it too tightly and you miss the feedback loops that matter most. Draw it too loosely and the model becomes unworkable <a name="apa-meadows-tis-citation-5"></a>([Meadows, 2008](#apa-meadows-tis-reference)).

Universal Cake implicitly draws its boundary at human technology and human wellbeing. But the document repeatedly gestures beyond that boundary — to the languages of non-human creatures, to ecological systems, to the full scale of life on earth. And the Sindermann fish are not a metaphor for human edge communities. They are part of the same system. The health of the oceans, the health of human communities, and the health of the technology ecosystems those communities depend on are not analogous — they are continuous. The stressors that weaken fish populations and the stressors that weaken human communities operate through the same mechanisms: cumulative load, immune suppression, opportunistic failure. The legal causation problem is the same in both domains. The clean water argument is the same in both domains.

This convergence is not accidental. It is what I found in *The Limits to Growth* — that **the human economy and the natural systems it depends on are part of the same system, and that models which draw the boundary between them will always produce dangerously optimistic forecasts, because the most important feedback loops cross that boundary** <a name="apa-limits-citation-2"></a>([Meadows et al., 1972](#apa-limits-reference)). 

> The costs of extraction do not disappear when you exclude them from the model. They accumulate outside the boundary and eventually re-enter the system as catastrophic surprises.



> Universal Cake should state its system boundary explicitly — and then state clearly that it is choosing to expand it.

Something like: **Universal Cake begins with human technology, but it does not end there**. The same questions — who can use this, who is excluded, can it last, what does it cost the world around it — apply at every scale, from a single application to the health of the living systems with which we share the planet.

The document is already asking these questions at the largest scale. It should say so, clearly, and explain why the boundary expansion matters: because **the feedback loops that will determine whether technology serves human flourishing or undermines it cross every boundary we might try to draw.**

---

## Summary recommendations

In order of leverage, highest first:

**State the paradigm shift explicitly.**

Universal Cake is not asking for better features. It is asking for a different goal — a different answer to the question "what is technology for?" Say that, clearly and early. It changes how everything else in the document is read.

**Name the feedback loops.**

* The Virtuous Tornado is named
* **The exclusion decay loop**
* **The knowledge preservation loop**
* **and the bridging capital loop are not.**

Name them and they become tools that practitioners can consciously choose to run or interrupt.

**Teach stocks and flows as a design principle.**

The insight that wellbeing is a stock that builds slowly and depletes faster than it builds is the most important practical implication of the clean water argument. State it as a principle: **design for the right stocks, not for the right immediate results**. This reframes every dimension of Universal Cake from a compliance requirement into a long-term investment logic.

**Map the dimensions, do not just list them.** The seven dimensions of Universal Cake are a list. They should be a map — showing **which are foundational stocks**, **which are flows**, **which reinforce each other**, and **where the dependencies run**. Accessibility is upstream of everything.

Interoperability enables resilience.

Transparency enables sustainability.

Draw this out.

**State and expand the system boundary.**

Universal Cake begins with human technology and human wellbeing. State clearly that it does not end there — and that the decision to expand the boundary beyond human systems is not philosophical decoration but a practical necessity, because

> the most important feedback loops in the system cross every boundary we try to draw.

---

## A closing thought

Christopher, you began with a one-page checklist in 1999 and arrived, twenty-five years later, at a framework that is — whether you intended it or not — a leverage point intervention.

> You are trying to change the goal function of a global industry. That is the right level of ambition.

It is also why the work is hard, and why the setbacks have been persistent.

Systems that are optimised for extraction resist redesign. The inertia of established stocks, the power of entrenched feedback loops, and the invisibility of paradigms to those inside them all work against the kind of change Universal Cake is proposing.

This is not a reason for despair. It is a reason for patience, for naming the loops clearly, for identifying the leverage points precisely, and for understanding that the work of cleaning the water is never finished — only continued.

The cake, as you put it, is mostly baked. What it needs now is a clearer recipe — one that shows not just what the ingredients are, but how they interact, which ones are load-bearing, and why the whole is reliably worth more than the sum of its parts.

I look forward to the next version.
