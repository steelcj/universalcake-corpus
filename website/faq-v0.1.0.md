## What is Universal Cake?

Universal Cake is a framework for thinking about technology in human terms. It evaluates technology across seven interdependent dimensions — accessibility, sustainability, interoperability, transparency, resilience, human well-being, and knowledge preservation — rather than purely technical criteria like speed, features, and price.

It is not a checklist. It is an attempt to change what technology is for.

---

## Is Universal Cake a product or a framework?

Both. The framework is a set of principles and questions for evaluating technology. The products — accessible themes, websites, and content pipeline tools — are built using the framework as their design constraint. The framework guides what we build; what we build validates the framework.

---

## How is this different from WCAG?

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is a technical standard that defines specific conformance criteria for web accessibility. It is valuable and necessary work. Universal Cake is not a replacement for WCAG.

The difference is scope. WCAG asks whether a specific piece of web content meets defined accessibility criteria. Universal Cake asks a broader set of questions: Can people use this in their language? On their device? With their bandwidth? Will their investment in it survive the next decade? Who governs the system? Who gets excluded when every product makes the same "reasonable" decision?

WCAG operates at the parameter level. Universal Cake operates at the level of goals and paradigms — asking what the system is organised to achieve, not just whether it meets a checklist.

---

## What does "accessibility first" actually mean?

It means accessibility is the starting point of every design decision, not a feature added after the product is built. If a person cannot use the system — because of language, disability, device, bandwidth, or cost — then nothing else about the system matters to that person.

Accessibility is not one dimension among seven. It is upstream of everything else. Without it, the other six dimensions cannot be reached.

---

## What does "language is infrastructure" mean?

Most technology treats language as a cosmetic layer — a translation to apply on top of a finished product. Universal Cake treats language as load-bearing infrastructure, like roads and power grids. If language infrastructure is weak, entire communities are structurally excluded regardless of their willingness to engage.

Around 380 million people speak English natively — roughly 5% of the world's population — yet 54% of websites are in English. A system launched in one language, optimised for one community, produces knowledge that reflects only that community's experience. Language infrastructure determines who can participate and who cannot.

## Who is Universal Cake for?

Anyone who wants to make better choices about the technology they use, build, or depend on. That includes:

- **Individuals** choosing tools for personal or professional use
- **Disability organisations** that need tools built for their communities, not adapted after
- **Government and institutional clients** with multilingual and accessibility mandates
- **Organisations** that produce enduring knowledge or need to own their technology stack
- **Builders** — developers, designers, and architects who want to think more carefully about what they are creating and who it serves

The framework does not require technical expertise. Its most important questions are human ones.

---

## Why does sovereignty matter for accessibility?

Accessibility decisions are governance decisions. If someone else controls the platform, someone else decides who gets in. A platform that adopts convenience-first design can remove access features. A vendor that changes terms or pricing can make tools unaffordable for the disability organisations that depend on them. A managed service that hides decision-making makes accessibility compliance unverifiable.

A system that surrenders authority to external actors cannot guarantee accessibility for the people who depend on it most. Sovereignty — the ability to govern, rebuild, and exit — is not separate from accessibility. It is a prerequisite.

## What is the "80/20 lie"?

The technology industry routinely applies the Pareto principle — the "80/20 rule" — to justify designing for a comfortable majority and ignoring everyone else. But Pareto's original observation was about wealth concentration in 1890s Italy. It was never a design principle. The "80%" being served was never 80% of the world. It was 80% of a narrow, usually English-speaking market on modern hardware with fast internet.

When every product independently ignores 20% of its potential users, and each draws its target market from roughly the same centre of the bell curve, the people at the edges are not excluded from one thing. They are excluded from most things. This is compounded digital exclusion, and it is the structural output of the 80/20 rule applied at scale.

## What is the Virtuous Tornado?

A concept from Jutta Treviranus (2019) at OCAD University. It describes the continuous, iterative process of asking "who are we missing?" and expanding inclusive design practices to reach them. The dynamic creates systems that grow stronger and more capable with each cycle — the opposite of the conventional approach, which optimises for the centre and becomes more fragile over time.

---

## What is the rebuildability test?

A single question proposed as a governance gate: "If this system disappeared tomorrow, could we recreate its essential function ourselves?" If the answer is no, you do not own what you depend on. Applied regularly and honestly, this question converts invisible dependency into a visible signal and limits lock-in at the point of adoption rather than at the point of crisis.

## How do I use Universal Cake?

Start by asking the guiding questions whenever you are evaluating a tool or building a system:

- Who can use this? Who cannot?
- Does it work in the languages of the people who need it?
- Will this still work in ten years?
- Can I take my data with me if I leave?
- Can I understand what this system is doing?
- Does this system support or undermine wellbeing?
- What happens if the organisation behind it disappears?
- Does it work for people with different abilities, languages, and devices?
- Who is still being left out?

These questions do not always have easy answers. But asking them changes the conversation.

## Where did the name come from?

A few years before the framework began, Christopher Steel and some artist friends started a communications company. They came up with the name Universal Cake, designed an actual box for the product, and then could never quite figure out what should go in it. The company did not survive. The name did. When Christopher started compiling his checklist of qualities that mattered for technology, the name fit perfectly — a cake is made of ingredients, and no single ingredient makes a cake. It takes all of them, in the right relationship, to produce something worth having.
