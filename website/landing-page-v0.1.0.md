## Technological agency through accessibility

Universal Cake is a framework for thinking about technology in human terms. It began in 1999 as a personal checklist — a set of questions about what actually matters when choosing the tools we depend on. It has since grown into a framework for evaluating technology across the dimensions that determine whether it serves people or excludes them.

> Technology that **builds, replenishes, and bridges** rather than **extracts, consumes, and diminishes.**

---

## The problem

The technology industry designed for a small minority of the world, gave that minority a name — "the majority" — and then applied a rule about Italian land ownership from the 1890s to justify ignoring everyone else.

- **~5%** of the world speaks English natively, yet **54%** of websites are in English
- **7,170** living languages; **44%** now endangered
- **1.3 billion** people live with significant disabilities worldwide
- Economic losses from excluding persons with disabilities: **3–7% of GDP** (ILO)

The cost is not in the people. It is in the systems that exclude them.

When every product independently ignores 20%, **the same people lose everything**. A person who speaks a minority language, uses older hardware, has low bandwidth, and has a disability is not excluded from one thing. They are excluded from nearly everything. Each individual decision seemed "reasonable." The cumulative effect is near-total exclusion from the digital infrastructure on which modern life depends.

---

## Seven dimensions

Universal Cake evaluates technology across seven interdependent dimensions:

| Dimension | What it asks |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Accessibility** | Can people actually use this — in their language, on their device, with their abilities? |
| **Sustainability** | Can this last without burning out its maintainers or its users? |
| **Interoperability** | Can your data move freely? Can you leave? |
| **Transparency** | Can you understand what the system is doing? |
| **Resilience** | Can this survive disruption — a company closing, a policy change? |
| **Human well-being** | Does this support or undermine the people who depend on it? |
| **Knowledge preservation** | Will your information still be readable in twenty years? |

These are not a flat list. They are a map of dependencies. Accessibility is upstream of everything else — without it, the other six cannot be reached. Human well-being is the purpose that gives them all their meaning.

---

## The approach

**Accessibility is the starting point.** Not a feature added later. Not a compliance checkbox. Accessibility is the door to the house. Without it, nothing else matters.

**Language is infrastructure.** Not cosmetic — load-bearing, like roads and power grids. A system launched in one language, optimised for one community, produces knowledge that reflects only that community's experience.

**Innovation lives at the edge.** The people at the margins cover 80% of the knowledge terrain (Treviranus, 2019). Transitional zones between ecosystems produce the greatest richness and capabilities found nowhere else (Odum, 1953). We build for the edges, and the centre benefits from what we learn there.

**Sovereignty is a prerequisite.** If someone else controls the platform, someone else decides who gets in. A system that surrenders authority to external actors cannot guarantee accessibility for the people who depend on it most.

---

## What we build

- Accessible themes, websites, and content pipeline tools
- Designed for real-world hardware, bandwidth, and language diversity
- Open standards and open formats throughout — no vendor lock-in
- User agency as the primary design constraint

Every collaborator must embrace accessibility as the foundational principle. This is not a policy preference — it is a condition of participation.

## Origin

In 1999, Christopher Steel needed website software for a community that included people with disabilities, people who did not read or write English, and people using older hardware. He wrote a checklist of qualities that mattered most. Accessibility moved to the top — not as a political statement but as a logical one.

Evaluating the options against that list, he found Plone: multilingual, open source, accessible from the ground up. Plone went on to be adopted by the United Nations, the FBI, and governments on multiple continents. Most of the software that competed with it is gone. Plone is still here. The list worked.

The organisations that adopted Plone had exactly the same requirements as that original checklist. They needed systems that worked across languages, across abilities, and across decades. The list was not unusual. It was just an honest assessment of what humans actually need from technology.

## Guiding questions

The simplest way to use Universal Cake is to ask these questions whenever you are evaluating a tool or building a system:

- Who can use this? Who cannot?
- Does it work in the languages of the people who need it?
- Will this still work in ten years?
- Can I take my data with me if I leave?
- Can I understand what this system is doing?
- Does this system support or undermine wellbeing?
- What happens if the organisation behind it disappears?
- Does it work for people with different abilities, languages, and devices?
- Who is still being left out?

These questions shift the focus from "what can this do?" to "what does this do to people, and to the world they share?"

---

## The shift

From capability to consequence.
From the centre of the bell curve to its edges.
From the short list to the full picture.

Universal Cake does not reject performance, speed, or cost. It places them within the context of our real world — the world in which humans, our communities, and our natural resources are found.
